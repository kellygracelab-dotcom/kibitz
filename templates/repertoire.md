# Opening repertoire

> This is a starter repertoire for roughly 1000-1600, not a recommendation for
> every player. Replace it once you know what kind of positions you like.
> Evaluations are from White's point of view; closer to zero is better for
> Black. Verify them with your own engine - do not trust a number in a file.

The selection principle is **self-overlap**: as few independent systems as
possible, so one idea learned pays off in several openings. The goal is not an
advantage out of the opening. It is reaching a position you understand by move
10 without burning clock.

## As White: 1.e4

### Main line - Italian Game, quiet variation

```
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5 4.c3 Nf6 5.d3
```

The point is **5.d3, not 5.d4**. That is the Giuoco Pianissimo: almost no
sharp theory, and in its place a ready-made setup eight moves deep.

Setup order: `0-0`, `Re1`, `Nbd2`, `Bb3`, `Nf1`, `Ng3`. Then play on the
kingside. You are not memorising lines; you are assembling the same structure
every game.

### Everything else

| Opponent plays | You play | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Sicilian `1...c5` | `2.c3` | Alapin: prepare d4 and take the centre |
| French `1...e6` | `2.d4 d5 3.e5` | Advance |
| Caro-Kann `1...c6` | `2.d4 d5 3.e5` | Advance |
| Scandinavian `1...d5` | `2.exd5 Qxd5 3.Nc3` | Gain a tempo on the queen |
| Petrov `1...e5 2.Nf3 Nf6` | `3.Nxe5 d6 4.Nf3` | Main line |

**The overlap that matters:** the French and the Caro-Kann after `3.e5` give
the *same* pawn structure - a locked chain, White pressing on the kingside,
Black hitting d4 and preparing `...c5`. One set of plans covers two openings.

## As Black

| Against | You play | Setup |
|---|---|---|
| `1.e4` | `1...e5` | The same Italian structures, from the other side |
| `1.d4` | Queen's Gambit Declined | `...d5`, `...e6`, `...Nf6`, `...Be7`, `0-0` |

**The second and more important overlap:** you play `1.e4` as White and answer
`1...e5` as Black, so you study the same positions from both sides. Every game
as Black teaches you something about playing White. That is roughly twice the
learning rate of an unrelated repertoire.

## Against 1.e4 when White avoids the Italian

### Scotch - `3.d4`

```
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 exd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6
```

**Black's whole idea is to play `...d5`.** Everything serves it: `...Nf6` hits
e4, the bishop goes to `b4` or `c5`, castle, then `...d5`. Once it lands, the
position is equal and your pieces are free.

Do not hold the centre with `3...d6`: White answers `4.d5`, the c6 knight has
no square, and engines suggest retreating it to b8. Four moves played, position
worse than the start.

White's knight on d4 looks strong, but White no longer has a pawn in the
centre and the knight is a target. That is your compensation for e5.

Simpler at club level: `4...Bc5` instead of `4...Nf6`. Same idea, more obvious.

### Vienna - `2.Nc3`

`2...Nf6`. Often transposes into something familiar.

If White continues `3.f4` (Vienna Gambit), take: `3...exf4`. This is the best
move and Black stands better. After `4.d4 Qh4+ 5.Ke2` you are in the main line
and the check is correct there.

### King's Gambit - `2.f4`

`2...exf4`, take the pawn.

**`...Qh4+` is only playable when there is no knight on f3.** After
`2...exf4 3.Nf3` the check simply loses the queen to `Nxh4`. Knight on c3, the
check is strong; knight on f3, it is a disaster. Check every time rather than
playing it from memory.

### Ruy Lopez - `3.Bb5`

`3...Nf6`, the Berlin. Minimal theory, solid position.

## What not to do

- Do not memorise long lines. Your opponent will leave theory almost every
  game, and knowing twelve moves does not help if the plan is unclear.
- Do not change your repertoire after a loss. Losses are almost never caused
  by the opening. Check with the engine before touching this file.
